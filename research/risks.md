# Sovereign, currency and pension-system failure risks — case studies and lessons

**Status:** Compiled 2026-09-09. Facts and figures are sourced as indicated; where a widely-cited figure
could not be pinned to a single primary document in this pass it is marked **ESTIMATE/approx**.

## 1. Zimbabwe — hyperinflation, dollarisation, re-conversion, ZiG (2000–2024)

**2008 hyperinflation.** Zimbabwe's hyperinflation peaked in **November 2008**, with the IMF's widely
cited estimate (Hanke & Kwok, drawing on IMF working paper analysis) putting **peak monthly inflation at
79.6 billion percent** (year-on-year inflation effectively unmeasurable/quintillions of percent). The
central bank stopped publishing official statistics before the peak; independent estimates (Hanke-Kwok)
are the standard reference.

**2009 dollarisation.** In February 2009 Zimbabwe formally adopted a multi-currency system (US dollar,
rand, etc.), abandoning the Zimbabwe dollar. Local-currency pensions, life-insurance policies and bank
balances were converted to USD at rates that, in practice, reduced them to near zero — pensioners who
had saved for decades received payouts worth a few US dollars or cents.

**Smith Commission (2015–2017).** President Mugabe appointed the **Justice George Smith Commission of
Inquiry** in July 2015 to investigate the loss of value of pension and insurance benefits over
1996(1999)–2015 (with a focus on 2000–Feb 2009). Public hearings ran Oct–Dec 2015 across provincial
capitals; the Commission examined 11 life insurers, 9 funeral insurers, 4 independent pension
administrators, 15 standalone pension funds, NSSA and the Government Pension Agency. Its report was
published in **March 2017**. Key finding: the loss of value was caused mainly by **macroeconomic,
regulatory and institutional failure** (currency collapse, lack of inflation-indexation, weak regulation)
rather than fund-level malpractice — dollarisation in 2009 merely *revealed* losses that had already
occurred during the preceding decade of hyperinflation. Zimbabwe subsequently passed **SI 162 of 2023**
(Pensions and Provident Funds (Compensation for Loss of Pre-2009 Value of Pension Benefits) Regulations)
to try to compensate affected pensioners — widely criticised as "too little, too late" given the amounts
involved were trivial relative to the original losses.

**2019 — Statutory Instrument 33 (SI 33 of 2019).** In February/March 2019 Zimbabwe reintroduced a local
currency (RTGS dollars). SI 33 of 2019 provided that **all RTGS/FCA "nostro" balances that had been
recorded in USD were converted to RTGS dollars at 1:1**, then let to float — the new currency
immediately began depreciating, so anyone who had rebuilt US-dollar savings (even electronically, inside
the formal banking system) after 2009 saw them redenominated into a fast-depreciating local currency
overnight, by government decree.

**2024 — ZiG.** On **5 April 2024**, via Statutory Instrument 60 of 2024, Zimbabwe launched a new
gold-backed currency, the **Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG)**, replacing the Zimbabwean dollar (RTGS dollar) at a
conversion rate of **2,498.7242 ZWL : 1 ZiG** (RBZ announcement, 6 April 2024). This was Zimbabwe's
**sixth currency change since 2008** — the fourth attempt in 15 years to stabilise the currency after
repeated collapses.

**What protected savers**: hard assets (property, livestock), foreign currency held physically or in
accounts *outside* Zimbabwe, and (for those with access) offshore investment portfolios. Onshore
foreign-currency deposits were not safe — they were converted by government decree in 2019.

Sources: [IMF WP — Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe (Hanke & Kwok analysis)](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/Hyperinflation-in-Zimbabwe); [Veritas Zim — Smith Commission report / Finance Minister's speech](https://www.veritaszim.net/node/2403); [Newsday — Smith Commission public hearings, 2015](https://www.newsday.co.zw/2015/10/public-hearings-on-insurance-pension-value-conversion-begin/); [Veritas Zim — SI 162 of 2023](https://www.veritaszim.net/node/6589); [RBZ — SI 33 of 2019 text](https://www.rbz.co.zw/documents/acts/SIs/RTGS--S.I.-33of-2019.pdf); [Wikipedia — Zimbabwean ZiG](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwean_ZiG); [IPEC Zimbabwe](https://www.ipec.co.zw/).

## 2. Argentina — default, corralito, pesification, AFJP nationalisation, 2023 inflation

**2001 default and corralito.** Argentina defaulted on **~US$95bn** of sovereign debt in December 2001 —
at the time the largest sovereign default in history. Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo imposed the
**"corralito"** — a freeze on bank withdrawals, initially limited to ~250 pesos/week.

**Pesification (Jan 2002).** Dollar-denominated bank deposits were forcibly converted ("pesified") into
pesos at **1.40 pesos per US dollar**, while the peso subsequently free-floated and devalued past **4
pesos per dollar** on the open market — meaning dollar savers who had their money trapped in Argentine
banks recovered only roughly a third to a half of the dollar value they thought they had (the exact loss
depends on the comparison date, commonly cited as **~60–70% loss** in real dollar terms). Dollar loans
owed *by* Argentines to banks were converted at a more favourable 1:1, an "asymmetric pesification" that
devastated bank balance sheets.

**2008 AFJP nationalisation.** In October–November 2008, President Cristina Fernández de Kirchner's
government nationalised Argentina's private, individually-funded pension system (the **AFJPs**),
transferring roughly **US$24 billion** in individual retirement account assets (some sources cite ~US$30bn
depending on valuation date) into the state pay-as-you-go system (ANSES). The Senate approved the measure
on **20/21 November 2008**. Around 9.5 million workers were covered; the funds had already lost about
17.5% of their value (~US$8.1bn) in the year before nationalisation amid the global financial crisis.
Private, capitalisation-based retirement savings simply ceased to exist as personal property — they
became a claim on the state's future ability/willingness to pay a pension.

**2023 inflation.** Argentina's annual inflation reached **211%** in 2023 (among the highest in the
world that year), continuing a decades-long pattern of currency debasement eroding both wages and the
now-nationalised pension system's real value.

**What protected savers**: assets held abroad (offshore bank accounts, foreign brokerage accounts,
foreign real estate), and to a lesser extent domestic real assets (property) — but the clearest lesson is
that being domiciled in Argentina, in Argentine institutions, in any currency, has repeatedly not been
enough.

Sources: [IMF — Lessons from the crisis in Argentina](https://www.imf.org/external/np/pdr/lessons/100803.pdf); [Wikipedia — Corralito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corralito); [AEI — Resolution of Argentina's financial crisis](https://www.aei.org/articles/resolution-of-argentinas-financial-crisis/); [NPR — Argentina to nationalize pensions](https://www.npr.org/2008/10/31/96369325/argentina-to-nationalize-pensions); [Al Jazeera — Argentina passes pension takeover](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2008/11/21/argentina-passes-pension-takeover); [France24 — Senate approves pension funds takeover](https://www.france24.com/en/20081121-senate-approves-pension-funds-takeover-argentina).

## 3. Greece — repeated public pension cuts (2010–2018)

Under three successive EU/IMF/ECB bailout programmes, Greek public pensions were cut by law more than a
dozen times between 2010 and 2016; cumulative reductions for many pensioners are commonly cited at
**roughly 40%** (range ~25–45% depending on pension level and which cuts are counted), alongside sharply
raised retirement ages. Greece also imposed **capital controls in 2015** during the acute crisis phase
(daily ATM withdrawal limits), showing that even inside the eurozone — with no exchange-rate risk —
a sovereign can restrict access to bank deposits when a debt crisis is severe enough.

Sources: [ESM — Greece financial assistance](https://www.esm.europa.eu/assistance/greece).

## 4. Cyprus — 2013 bank deposit bail-in

In March–July 2013, as part of a €10bn EU/IMF bailout, Cyprus imposed **the first (and to date only)
formal depositor bail-in in a developed banking system**:
- **Laiki Bank** (Cyprus Popular Bank) was wound up: insured deposits (<€100,000) transferred to Bank of
  Cyprus; **uninsured deposits above €100,000 were effectively wiped out**.
- **Bank of Cyprus**: uninsured deposits above €100,000 were converted into equity in the recapitalised
  bank at a final haircut of **47.5%** (an initial 37.5% conversion was later topped up to 47.5% once the
  Central Bank of Cyprus finalised the number, completed 30 July 2013).
- Deposits below the €100,000 insured limit were untouched at both banks.
- Capital controls (limits on withdrawals and transfers) were imposed and lasted roughly two years.

Sources: [Central Bank of Cyprus](https://www.centralbank.cy/); [Yale/EliScholar — Laiki Bank and Bank of Cyprus restructuring 2013](https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1533&context=journal-of-financial-crises); [Motley Fool — Bank of Cyprus depositors to lose 47.5%](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/07/29/bank-of-cyprus-depositors-to-lose-475-of-savings-o.aspx).

## 5. Lebanon — banking collapse, 2019–2023

Since October 2019, Lebanese banks have imposed informal, non-legislated capital controls: US-dollar
depositors could not withdraw their actual dollars, only being paid out (if at all) in Lebanese pounds
at the official peg, or in restricted "**lollar**" withdrawals at a fraction of the real market value.
The Lebanese pound **lost roughly 98% of its value** against the dollar through the crisis. Estimates of
how much of their US-dollar deposit value savers ultimately recovered vary widely (roughly **15–30%** in
practical purchasing terms is a commonly cited range), and various restructuring plans have proposed
formal haircuts as high as **~90%** on government debt / bank sovereign exposure, with a further ~18%+
haircut on banks' sovereign exposure needed for recapitalisation. By 2023 Lebanon's debt-to-GDP exceeded
**180%**. No formal, legislated resolution/haircut framework had been enacted as of the period covered by
available sources — the "lollar" system operated as a de facto, unlegislated haircut imposed by the banks
themselves.

**Lesson**: a US dollar sitting in a domestic bank account in a country experiencing a banking crisis is
not the same asset as a US dollar held with an institution outside that country's banking system.

Sources: [World Bank — Lebanon overview](https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/lebanon); [S&P Global — Lebanon banking crisis](https://www.spglobal.com/marketintelligence/en/mi/research-analysis/lebanon-banking-crisis.html); [FDD — Crisis in Lebanon](https://www.fdd.org/analysis/2020/08/04/crisis-in-lebanon/).

## 6. Ghana — Domestic Debt Exchange Programme (DDEP), 2022–2023

Facing a debt crisis (debt-to-GDP well above sustainable levels, inflation over 50%), Ghana restructured
its domestic bonds in Dec 2022–2023. Key terms as they affected pension funds:
- Under a **Memorandum of Understanding (22 December 2022)** with labour unions, government agreed to
  **exempt pension funds from principal haircuts** and to preserve original terms on coupons/maturity for
  most pension holdings.
- In practice, roughly **GHS 29.6bn** of pension-fund-held Treasury, ESLA and Daakye bonds were
  restructured, with **coupon rates cut to 0% in 2023, 5% in 2024, then stepping up (to ~10%, then to the
  original ~20% coupon from 2025)** and maturities extended (~4 years average) — a real-terms loss via
  coupon suppression and extended duration even without a headline principal haircut.
- Individual retail investors and T-bill holders were **exempt** from the programme entirely; banks,
  insurers, mutual funds and asset managers (i.e. institutions, including pension funds) were the ones
  restructured — illustrating that **institutional/pension holdings of government debt are the first
  target of a domestic restructuring**, precisely because retail depositors are politically harder to
  touch.
- Estimated **net-present-value losses of roughly 30%** on the affected bonds even after the pension-fund
  carve-outs.

Sources: [Ministry of Finance, Ghana — DDEP updates](https://www.mofep.gov.gh/press-release/2023-02-28/domestic-debt-exchange-programme-updates); [MyJoyOnline — Haircuts in Ghana's DDEP explained](https://www.myjoyonline.com/dr-yakubu-abdul-salam-haircuts-in-ghanas-domestic-debt-exchange-programme-a-not-so-simple-explainer/); [NBC Ghana Trust — DDEP impact on pensions](https://nbcghanatrust.com/domestic-debt-exchange-program-and-impact-on-pension/); [IMF — Ghana country page](https://www.imf.org/en/Countries/GHA).

## 7. Brief mentions — Venezuela, Russia 1998, Turkey, Nigeria, Sri Lanka

- **Venezuela**: entered hyperinflation from ~2016–2018, with annual inflation exceeding **1,000,000%**
  in 2018 (IMF estimate); price controls and state mismanagement of the currency destroyed the value of
  bolívar-denominated savings and pensions almost entirely. One of the starkest modern hyperinflation
  cases alongside Zimbabwe.
- **Russia (1998)**: the government devalued the ruble and **defaulted on domestic (ruble-denominated)
  debt** on 17 August 1998 — a reminder that local-currency debt is not automatically "safe" for a
  government to service, and that domestic-currency default, while rarer than foreign-currency default,
  does happen.
- **Turkey**: the lira has been in a multi-year depreciation crisis; it fell a further ~20% in 2025,
  reaching a record low around **42.4 lira/US$** by December 2025 — an example of persistent currency
  depreciation (rather than a single default event) eroding local-currency retirement savings over time.
- **Nigeria**: the naira was devalued sharply in 2024 as part of FX-liberalisation reforms, losing
  **~43%** of its value against the US dollar that year.
- **Sri Lanka (2022)**: suspended external debt payments in April 2022 and formally defaulted in May
  2022 (debt-to-GDP ~126%). Its **domestic debt restructuring avoided a headline capital haircut on
  pension funds** (the Employees' Provident Fund/EPF and Employees' Trust Fund/ETF), but achieved a
  similar economic effect by **cutting the interest rate on pension-fund-held government bonds from
  20%+ to 12%, then to 9%** through 2025 — or requiring funds to accept a **30% haircut** as an
  alternative to a higher 30% tax rate on interest (vs 14% otherwise). As in Ghana, pension funds (the
  main pool of "captive" domestic savings) bore a large share of the fiscal adjustment.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Hyperinflation in Venezuela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_Venezuela); [Wikipedia — 1998 Russian financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Russian_financial_crisis); [IMF — Sri Lanka Sovereign Debt Restructuring Lessons (2025)](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/2025/175/article-A001-en.xml); [World Socialist Web Site — Sri Lanka to slash pension funds](https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/2023/07/05/cphl-j05.html).

## 8. South Africa — specific institutional and governance risks

### 8.1 South Africa has never defaulted on local-currency debt
South Africa has a long, genuinely distinguishing track record: it has **never defaulted on
rand-denominated (local-currency) government debt**. The one blemish on its sovereign credit history is
the **1985 foreign-debt standstill**: after P.W. Botha's "Rubicon" speech (15 August 1985) triggered a
~20% overnight rand collapse and a foreign capital flight, South Africa announced on **1 September 1985**
a **standstill on principal repayments of ~US$13.6bn of foreign (mostly private-sector) debt**, while
continuing to service interest through a special account. This was a moratorium/rescheduling, not a
repudiation — the debt was serviced and the last rescheduled tranche was repaid by **2001** — and it
applied to *foreign* debt, not local-currency government bonds. The **Financial Rand** exchange-control
mechanism was reintroduced the same day.

### 8.2 Exchange control history
South Africa has operated exchange controls (in various forms, including the dual/financial rand system)
continuously since the 1960s-80s, tightened sharply around the 1985 standstill, and has been **gradually
liberalising** since 1994 — replacing blanket controls with the current framework of individual
allowances (Single Discretionary Allowance, Foreign Investment Allowance — see `offshore.md`) subject to
SARB oversight via Authorised Dealers. Full abolition of exchange controls has been discussed for decades
but not implemented; commentators (e.g. Jannie Rossouw) have periodically argued for the framework's
demise, but as of 2026 controls remain in place, only progressively loosened (allowances have
increased over time, and SARS/SARB clearance requirements simplified).

### 8.3 Prescribed assets debate
The idea of **"prescribed assets"** — legally compelling retirement funds to hold a minimum percentage
of government bonds or infrastructure investments — has recurred in ANC election manifestos since **2019**,
repeated in the **2024** manifesto (proposing "investigating" prescribed assets to fund infrastructure).
As of the 2026 Budget cycle, **no prescribed-assets law has been enacted**. Instead, the amended
**Regulation 28** (effective 2022, further consultation ongoing into 2025) took the *voluntary* route:
retirement funds may allocate **up to 45%** to infrastructure (raised from 25% under the old
"other assets" limits) without being compelled to. The debate is politically live (recurs whenever ANC
election messaging needs a populist infrastructure-funding answer) but has not translated into legal
compulsion as of September 2026.

### 8.4 PIC/GEPF governance failures
The **Public Investment Corporation (PIC)** manages the bulk of GEPF's ~R2.7 trillion in assets. A
presidential **Commission of Inquiry into the PIC**, chaired by retired Judge President **Lex Mpati**
(with former Reserve Bank Governor Gill Marcus and asset manager Emmanuel Lediga), investigated PIC
governance and reported in **2020**. Findings included:
- **Steinhoff**: the PIC's then-CEO was found to have improperly structured a loan/investment
  transaction to avoid board-level approval thresholds; after Steinhoff's December 2017 accounting-fraud
  collapse (share price fell **>80%**), the GEPF/PIC ultimately **wrote off more than R10 billion**.
- **Ayo Technology Solutions**: the PIC invested **R4.3 billion** in Ayo's 2017 listing (essentially the
  only investor), later pursuing legal action to recover ~R4.2bn amid allegations the investment was
  improperly motivated and Ayo's share price collapsed.
- **VBS Mutual Bank**: a smaller PIC-adjacent exposure (~R475 million) connected to the broader VBS
  looting scandal (VBS itself, not a GEPF holding, collapsed in 2018 after ~R2bn was looted, mostly
  affecting municipal deposits rather than GEPF directly, but implicating overlapping PIC-linked
  individuals).
- Systemic finding: **transactions were pushed through without proper due diligence or governance
  process**, with excessive power concentrated in the PIC CEO position; recommendations included that the
  PIC board chair should not be a "political person," and led to a revised PIC Memorandum of
  Incorporation and operating-model changes.
- **SOE exposure**: the PIC/GEPF also carries large, concentrated exposure to South African
  state-owned-enterprise debt and equity (Eskom, Transnet, etc.) as part of its ~90% domestic asset
  allocation — a structural concentration risk distinct from any single scandal.

### 8.5 The GEP Law guarantee
GEPF benefits are defined by statute — the **Government Employees Pension Law, 1996 (Proclamation 21 of
1996, as amended, "the GEP Law")** — not by a funding-level-contingent trust deed alone. Because benefits
are set in law, a member is entitled to the full statutory benefit **regardless of the Fund's investment
performance**; the state, as guarantor/employer, is legally obligated to make up any shortfall. As of the
31 March 2024 valuation the Fund does not need such a top-up (119% funded on a best-estimate basis — see
`macro.md` §11), but the guarantee is the legal backstop, distinguishing GEPF's defined-benefit promise
from a defined-contribution/living-annuity arrangement where the member alone bears investment risk.

### 8.6 What has protected South African savers so far
- **The rand has always remained a floating, independently-managed currency** (unlike Zimbabwe's forced
  currency changes) — SA has not experienced a currency redenomination event.
- **No local-currency sovereign default** and no domestic deposit bail-in or freeze (unlike Argentina,
  Cyprus, Lebanon) — SA banks have not experienced a "corralito" or lollar-style freeze.
- **A functioning, internationally-audited, independent central bank** (SARB) with (as of 2025) a
  credible commitment to a lower inflation anchor, plus (as of Oct 2025) exit from the FATF grey list —
  both improving external perceptions of institutional quality.
- **The GEP Law's statutory guarantee** for GEPF members specifically.
- For those diversifying: **offshore-held foreign-currency assets** (see `offshore.md`) have been the
  single most consistent protection observed across every case study above — the common thread in
  Zimbabwe, Argentina, Cyprus, Lebanon and Ghana is that **onshore assets, even foreign-currency onshore
  assets, were all vulnerable to conversion, freezing or restructuring by decree**, while assets held with
  foreign-domiciled custodians/institutions were not reachable by the domestic authority.

Sources: [Washington Post — SA freezes foreign debt payments, 1985](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1985/09/02/south-africa-freezes-foreign-debt-payments/91d1253c-8312-4fa1-badb-ea6fa34bdcfd/); [Wikipedia — Financial rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_rand); [Justice.gov.za — Exchange control history (Rand Commission report excerpt)](https://www.justice.gov.za/commissions/comm_rand/rand_final_minority_report/part_3a.pdf); [Moonstone — Proposed Reg 28 amendment: are prescribed assets coming back?](https://www.moonstone.co.za/proposed-regulation-28-amendment-are-prescribed-assets-coming-back/); [Moneyweb — ANC to pursue prescribed assets plan after vote](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/south-africa/anc-to-pursue-prescribed-assets-plan-after-vote/); [PMG — Mpati Commission of Inquiry Report: PIC briefing](https://pmg.org.za/committee-meeting/31674/); [Daily Maverick — the high-profile deals that sank the PIC's governance](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2020-03-16-the-high-profile-deals-that-sank-the-pics-governance-processes/); [Moneyweb — GEPF implements Mpati Commission recommendations](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/south-africa/gepf-implements-a-number-of-mpati-commission-recommendations/); [Polity — PIC board welcomes Mpati Commission report](https://www.polity.org.za/article/pic-board-welcomes-report-of-commission-of-inquiry-into-its-affairs-disciplinary-action-to-be-taken-against-those-implicated-2020-03-20); [GEPF — GEP Law and Rules](https://www.gepf.co.za/gepf-law-and-rules/); [GEPF — GEP Law text (PDF, 11 Sept 2024)](https://www.gepf.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/GEP-LAW@-11-SEPTEMBER-2024-1.pdf).

## 9. Lessons for a GEPF member weighing "stay vs resign"

1. **A defined-benefit promise is only as strong as (a) the currency it's paid in and (b) the guarantor
   behind it.** GEPF's statutory guarantee (GEP Law) and SA's clean local-currency default record are
   real, structural advantages relative to Zimbabwe's or Argentina's pension members — but they are not
   an argument for zero currency diversification, because the rand itself has depreciated steadily for
   decades even without a crisis event.
2. **Onshore ≠ safe, even in foreign currency.** Every case study (Zimbabwe's 2019 RTGS conversion,
   Argentina's pesification, Cyprus's bail-in, Lebanon's "lollars") shows that money — even dollars —
   held with a domestic institution can be redenominated, frozen or haircut by decree. True currency
   diversification requires assets held with an offshore-domiciled custodian, not just an onshore
   "dollar" account or a rand-denominated feeder fund.
3. **Domestic pension funds are usually the first target when a sovereign needs money it can't borrow
   elsewhere** — Argentina's AFJPs, Ghana's DDEP, Sri Lanka's EPF/ETF, and South Africa's own recurring
   "prescribed assets" debate are all instances of the same pattern: captive, large pools of
   institutionally-held domestic savings are politically easier to tap than either external creditors or
   individual retail depositors. This is a real, structural argument for having *some* wealth outside the
   domestic retirement-fund system and outside Regulation-28-constrained vehicles.
4. **A "hard" local-currency default is rare (SA has never had one, Russia 1998 is the exception, not the
   rule) — but slow currency debasement or high inflation achieves a similar erosion of real value over
   time without ever technically defaulting.** Zimbabwe/Argentina/Venezuela lost value through outright
   crisis; Turkey and (to a lesser, historically well-managed degree) South Africa itself lose real value
   more gradually through persistent above-target inflation and currency depreciation — the risk is
   different in speed, not in kind.
5. **Institutional governance quality matters as much as sovereign solvency.** GEPF/PIC's own governance
   failures (Steinhoff, Ayo, VBS, per the Mpati Commission) show that even a well-funded (119%),
   state-guaranteed fund can lose real member value through poor investment governance — a risk that
   exists independently of currency or sovereign-debt risk, and one a member cannot personally control
   while remaining in GEPF (though it can be mitigated by monitoring PIC/GEPF governance reform and by
   diversifying personal savings outside the Fund).
6. **South Africa's recent trajectory (FATF exit Oct 2025, first Fitch/S&P upgrades in ~20 years in
   2025/26, debt-to-GDP peaking) is genuinely improving** relative to the worst-case comparators in this
   file — SA is not Zimbabwe or Argentina, and the case studies above are cautionary extremes, not a
   prediction. The right reading is "diversify sensibly against tail risk," not "expect an imminent
   crisis."
